# Explainable Machine Learning for Urban Mobility Demand Prediction: A Big Data Analytics Approach

## All steps were implemented in a single Python notebook using pandas, NumPy, scikit-learn, XGBoost, SHAP, matplotlib and Joblib (Section 3.8). The notebook runs end-to-end in Google Colab in approximately 10–15 minutes on a standard CPU runtime and uses less than 2 GB of memory.

A.1 Source data. Citi Bike trip-history file for June 2013, obtained through Kaggle (Kaggle, n.d.); 577,703 trip records; original 2013 field names, standardised to the later naming convention in the notebook; underlying operational source: Citi Bike system data (Citi Bike, n.d.). The raw file was stored unchanged, and the cleaned and model-ready datasets were saved as separate files.

A.2 Cleaning. Start timestamps were parsed; unparseable timestamps, exact duplicates, missing start-station identifiers and implausible coordinates (latitude outside 40.4–41.0 or longitude outside −74.3 to −73.6) were checked for removal (none were found); trip durations outside one minute to twenty-four hours were flagged but not removed (344 records). End-of-trip fields were excluded from the modelling data (Table 4.1).

A.3 Aggregation. Departures were counted by start station and clock hour; stations with fewer than 100 departures in the month were excluded (334 of 337 stations retained); a complete hourly grid was built for every retained station, with empty station-hours coded as zero (240,814 station-hours; Table 4.3).

A.4 Feature engineering. Within each station, after sorting by time: lag_1, lag_24 and lag_168; roll_3 and roll_24 calculated on the series shifted by one hour; hour, day of week, weekend, morning-peak (07:00–09:59) and evening-peak (16:00–19:59) indicators; sine and cosine encodings of hour and day of week; station code, latitude, longitude and the training-period station mean (Table 4.4; Equations 1–5). Rows without 168 hours of history were dropped, leaving 184,702 observations.

A.5 Partitioning. Chronological 70:15:15 split: training 8 June 2013 00:00 to 24 June 03:00; validation 24 June 04:00 to 27 June 14:00; test 27 June 15:00 to 1 July 00:00 (Table 4.5). Scaling parameters and the station mean were fitted on the training block only.

A.6 Tuning. Time-series cross-validation with three expanding-window folds (TimeSeriesSplit, n_splits = 3) on the training block, scored by mean absolute error; search grids and selected configurations as reported in Table 4.6, with all configurations in Table B.1; fixed random seed (42) for all stochastic models; Random Forest fitted with 200 trees in both the search and the final fit, and XGBoost with 400 trees; all final models fitted on the training block only, with training time measured on a single processor core.

A.7 Evaluation. MAE, RMSE and R² on the validation and test blocks (Tables 4.7–4.8); naive 24-hour persistence benchmark (Table 4.9); MAE by temporal segment and observed-demand band (Tables 4.10–4.11); negative Linear Regression predictions clipped at zero before evaluation.

A.8 Explanation. Standardised Linear Regression coefficients; the upper three levels of the fitted Decision Tree; permutation importance on the test block for all four models (five repeats, random seed 42); TreeSHAP for Random Forest and XGBoost, including summary, dependence and one waterfall plot, computed on a random sample of 2,000 test station-hours, with the waterfall case defined as the test station-hour with the largest previous-hour demand at 18:00 (Tables 4.12–4.15; Figures 4.13–4.19).

A.9 Code availability. The Python notebook implementing Steps A.1–A.8 (Citi_Bike_Explainable_ML_June2013_FINAL.ipynb) accompanies this thesis. It reads the raw file in CSV, zip or gzip form, regenerates Tables 4.1–4.15 and 4.17 and Figures 4.1–4.19, and exports them together with the fitted models and the additional outputs in Appendix B. The reported results were generated in Google Colab with scikit-learn 1.6.1 and random seed 42, and the notebook saves the exact versions of all libraries in run_configuration.json. A different scikit-learn version can change the Random Forest results in the third decimal place (for example, a test MAE of 1.7134 instead of 1.7142 with scikit-learn 1.9) without affecting the rankings or conclusions; training times also depend on the hardware used.
